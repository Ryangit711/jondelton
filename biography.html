David Horton Elton's Biography

The Life of Grandpa D.H. Elton (1877–1963)

by Winston Elton

Our Grandpa David Horton Elton was an amazing man who led a long and interesting life of 86 years during eras of tremendous change in the world, from his birth in January 1877 in the West Midlands area northwest of London in England, to the growing Salt Lake City, Utah in the western U.S. as a youth, as a missionary in the Southern States at the turn of the last century, and as a young married man in early developing Alberta in Western Canada, raising his family there for fifty years, and finally “shuffling off this mortal coil” in Calgary after a brief retirement in Arizona, in 1963. His fourscore and six years spanned two world wars, half a dozen major economic downturns, and a dozen political upheavals. He and his spicy child bride Nora Afton Hauser raised five sparkling children in his chosen family community of Lethbridge, Alberta, where he has a city park named in his honor.

David Horton Elton was born in January 1877 a few miles southwest of Worcester, England, which is south of the larger western English cities of Liverpool, Birmingham and Manchester, and just a few miles east of the mountainous Welsh border. His parents were John Elton and Isabella Amelia Horton. His father, John, was born in 1817 in the same area, and John married Isabella in 1854, some years after his first wife died, when John was 37 years old and Isabella the tender age of 17. David was born when his father was 60 years old (probably 5 – 10 years older than the average life span for the time), the tenth of a family of twelve. David’s oldest siblings were twenty years older than he, and he had two younger sisters, twins, who were born two years after he was, and both died from smallpox on the same day when they were two years old.

His father John was converted at age 33 to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints (Mormon) in 1840 during a great missionary effort at that time, and died at age 63 from a heart attack, collapsing while talking in the street with friends, in 1880, when David was only three years old, so David never really had a father that he could remember. Most of David’s older brothers and sisters emigrated to the U.S. in the 1880s, and David came to Salt Lake City in 1890 with his mother and three next older brothers at age 13. David never forgot his English roots, although he left rural England at age 13, and probably never experienced English big city life.

The Salt Lake years were hard as a young man as they were not wealthy, and there were several significant severe economic downturns in his teens. David worked as a stonecutter for graveyard headstones, on several different smaller local Utah railroads, and at whatever other jobs he could get to help support he and his mother, Isabella. His older brothers had grown up and moved on, and David was the last to leave in 1898, when, at age 21, he went on a three year mission for his church to the Southern States Mission, or “Conference” as they were called at the time, in Florida, Georgia, Kentucky and Tennessee. He distinguished himself as the poet laureate of the mission as well as editor of the mission newspaper. While on his mission, he became good friends with Charles V.M. Hauser through the mission offices, and he met Charles daughter, the lovely young Nora Afton Hauser, at 17, six years younger than Grandpa, who would later become his wife.

Upon returning home from his mission in early 1901, he was called by then church President Lorenzo Snow to go to Canada to start a newspaper to help promote acceptance of the church in Southern Alberta. The pioneers of the church arrived there fourteen years earlier. The President told David that he needed to have a wife, so in the same month that he got back from his mission, he returned to Chattanooga, Tennessee, proposed to Afton Hauser, was married in the Salt Lake Temple and a few months later, moved to the then Canadian outback in Cardston, Alberta. Viva was born in 1902, Ursula (“Zuki”) was born in 1904, David in 1908, Earl Kitchener in 1915, and Dalton in 1920. David started various different small-town newspapers in Cardston and surrounding towns as editor and publisher and became a justice of the peace for a few years soon after he got to Cardston.

Ambitious, self-educated, and wanting to improve himself and his growing family needs, and with no previous college education, he articled as a law student, taking law school by correspondence and studying under a lawyer from Medicine Hat in 1910-1911, finally receiving his degree a few years later from the University of Alberta. Leaving the newspaper business, in his mid-adult years he became a successful lawyer, articling with Hugh B. Brown, judge and politician culminating when he first became a council member from the mid ’20s to the mid ’30s, then later Mayor of Lethbridge from the mid-30s through the Second World War. He retired to Arizona to live with his oldest daughter in 1955 at age 78, and returned to Calgary to live the last few years of his life with his son Kitchener in the early 1960s.
